=>>> AI Implementation in WaveTone =>>>

1> Core Framing
> AI in WaveTone assists moderation and user experience.
> AI does not control communication or user identity.
> The non-AI system remains the foundation, with AI as an optional layer.


2> AI Option A: Speech-to-Text with AI Profanity Classification

> Convert buffered audio to text using a lightweight speech-to-text model or API.
> Run the text through an ML-based profanity or toxicity classifier.
> Extract timestamps of offensive segments.
> Suppress only the detected profane segments.

Why this is safe
> Audio is already buffered (300–500 ms).
> AI only labels segments and does not store audio or identity data.

Academic framing
> “AI is used to enhance moderation accuracy while preserving latency and privacy constraints.”

Pros
> More accurate than keyword-based filtering
> Handles accents and word variations
> Detects contextual profanity

Cons
> Higher latency risk
> External API dependency (unless using a local model)
> Increased debugging complexity

Verdict
> Optional advanced feature, not part of the MVP


3> AI Option B: Disruptive Behavior Detection (No Speech Content)

What it does
> AI analyzes how users behave rather than what they say.
> Model inputs include:
> Speaking frequency
> Interruptions
> Volume spikes
> Long continuous monologues
> Only audio metadata is used, not speech content.

Use case
> Issue warnings for disruptive behavior even without profanity.
> Supports vote-kick and moderation systems.

Pros
> Privacy-preserving
> No speech recognition required
> Highly unique and academically defensible

Cons
> Requires behavior data over time
> Slightly abstract to explain initially

Verdict
> Strong and defensible AI add-on


4> AI Option C: Adaptive Moderation Thresholds

> AI dynamically adjusts moderation rules based on room context.

Examples
> Calm or study rooms use stricter profanity thresholds.
> Debate rooms allow slightly relaxed thresholds.

Pros
> Context-aware moderation
> Sophisticated behavior without heavy computation

Cons
> Requires clean and accurate room metadata
> Needs careful explanation to avoid confusion

Verdict
> Optional polish feature


=>>> Profanity Filtering (Non-AI, Safe Scope) =>>>

1> Option 1: Keyword-Based Speech Match (Dictionary)

How it works
> Convert audio to text using basic speech-to-text or phonetic matching.
> Match text against a predefined list of profane words.

Pros
> Simple to implement
> Predictable behavior
> Low computational cost

Cons
> Misses slang and modified words
> False positives (e.g., “bass” vs “ass”)
> Language dependent

When to use
> MVP implementation
> Academic demonstrations
> Controlled environments


2> Option 2: Client-Side Word Masking Using Timestamps

How it works
> Audio chunks are analyzed locally on the client.
> Profane segments are muted or replaced before playback.

Pros
> Lowest server load
> Strong privacy preservation
> Faster perceived response

Cons
> Client hardware dependent
> Harder to enforce consistency
> More vulnerable to bypass

When to use
> Privacy-first implementations
> Small-scale rooms


3> Option 3: Server-Side Segment Muting (Beep or Silence)

How it works
> Audio is buffered briefly on the server.
> Only the detected profane segment is replaced with a beep or low-volume noise.
> Clean audio stream is sent to peers.

Pros
> Centralized control
> Consistent moderation
> Easier logging using timestamps only

Cons
> Slight increase in latency
> Higher server bandwidth usage

When to use
> Best academic balance
> Most controllable and explainable approach


=>>> Final Recommendation =>>>

> Server-side segment muting should be used as the core profanity filtering mechanism.
> AI-based detection can be layered on top later for improved accuracy.
